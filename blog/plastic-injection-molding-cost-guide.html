Plastic Injection Molding Cost Guide for Small Businesses

May 14, 2026 5 min read

One of the first questions any small business asks before committing to injection molding is straightforward: what's it going to cost? The answer isn't a single number — it's a combination of tooling investment, per-part production costs, and the design decisions that influence both. Understanding how plastic injection molding cost breaks down puts you in a stronger position to budget accurately and avoid surprises.

At Ace's Injection Molding Inc. (AIM) in Bohemia, NY, we work with small businesses and startups every day. Many of our customers come in assuming injection molding is out of reach for their volume or budget. Most of the time, they're wrong — and the numbers prove it once we walk through the actual cost structure.

Tooling Costs: The Upfront Investment

The mold itself is typically the largest single expense in any injection molding project. Tooling costs vary widely depending on part complexity, mold material, number of cavities, and surface finish requirements.

For a simple, single-cavity aluminum mold — the kind used for low to moderate volumes — you might spend between $3,000 and $10,000. More complex parts with multiple cavities, side actions, or tight tolerances in hardened steel can run $15,000 to $50,000 or more. Production-grade multi-cavity steel molds for high-volume runs can exceed $100,000. To understand the full mold building process behind these numbers, read how injection molds are made.

The key point for small businesses: you don't always need the most expensive tool. A well-designed aluminum mold can produce thousands of parts with excellent quality, and it costs a fraction of what a full hardened steel tool would run. At AIM, we help customers choose the right tooling strategy for their actual volume needs — not the theoretical maximum.

Per-Part Costs: Where Injection Molding Shines

Once the mold is built, the per-part cost of injection molding drops dramatically compared to other manufacturing methods. This is where the process earns its reputation for cost-effectiveness.

Per-part pricing for injection molded components typically falls between $0.50 and $5.00 for most small to medium-sized parts, depending on material, cycle time, and part weight. For very simple parts in commodity resins like polypropylene or ABS, costs can be even lower.

Compare that to CNC machining the same part at $15 to $50 per unit, or 3D printing at $5 to $30 per unit with inferior material properties, and the economics become clear — even at relatively modest volumes of a few hundred parts.

The breakeven point where injection molding becomes cheaper than alternatives is often lower than people expect. For many parts, you start saving money after just 200 to 500 units.

What Affects Your Injection Molding Price?

Several factors influence both tooling and per-part costs. Understanding these gives you leverage to design smarter and budget more accurately:

  • Part complexity: Undercuts, thin walls, tight tolerances, and complex geometries all increase mold cost. Simpler parts mean simpler, cheaper molds.
  • Part size and weight: Larger parts require more material per shot, longer cycle times, and larger molds — all of which increase cost.
  • Material selection: Commodity resins (ABS, PP, PE) are inexpensive. Engineering-grade materials (nylon, polycarbonate, PEEK) cost significantly more per pound and may require specialized processing.
  • Surface finish: A textured or high-polish finish adds machining time to the mold. If cosmetics aren't critical, a standard finish saves money.
  • Number of cavities: A single-cavity mold costs less upfront but produces one part per cycle. Multi-cavity molds cost more but reduce per-part cost at higher volumes.
  • Tolerances: Standard tolerances are achievable with standard tooling. Tighter tolerances require more precise machining and potentially more quality checks.
  • Order quantity: Higher volumes spread the tooling cost across more parts, reducing the effective cost per unit.

How to Reduce Your Injection Molding Costs

Small businesses often have more control over their injection molding pricing than they realize. Here are practical strategies that make a real difference:

Design for manufacturability (DFM). The single most impactful thing you can do is work with your molder early in the design process. Small changes — adding draft angles, adjusting wall thickness, eliminating unnecessary undercuts — can reduce tooling cost by 20 to 40 percent without changing the part's function.

Choose the right tooling material. Not every mold needs to be hardened steel. Aluminum molds are faster to machine, cost less, and work perfectly for runs under 10,000 parts. For very low volumes, even 3D-printed mold inserts can be viable. Our P20 vs H13 mold steel comparison helps you understand the tradeoffs, and our mold making page covers all tooling options.

Start with a single-cavity mold. If your initial volumes are modest, a single-cavity tool keeps upfront costs low. You can always upgrade to a multi-cavity mold later when demand justifies the investment.

Use standard materials when possible. Unless your application demands specific properties — chemical resistance, high temperature tolerance, FDA compliance — a standard resin will do the job at a fraction of the cost.

Consolidate parts. If your assembly has multiple simple plastic components, a family mold that produces several parts in a single shot can save both tooling and per-part costs.

Plan for realistic volumes. Over-tooling for volumes you may never reach wastes money. A good molder will help you match your tooling investment to your actual market demand.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does injection molding cost for small businesses?

For small businesses, injection molding costs typically break down into two parts: tooling (the mold) and per-part production. A simple single-cavity aluminum mold can start around $3,000–$8,000, while complex steel molds range from $10,000–$50,000+. Per-part costs drop significantly at higher volumes — often $0.50–$5.00 per part depending on size, material, and complexity.

What's the biggest cost factor in injection molding?

Tooling — the mold itself — is usually the largest upfront investment. Mold cost is driven by complexity (number of cavities, undercuts, side actions), material (aluminum vs hardened steel), and surface finish requirements. However, for high-volume production, per-part material and cycle time costs become the dominant factor over the life of the project.

Can I reduce injection molding costs without sacrificing quality?

Yes. Common strategies include designing for manufacturability (DFM) to simplify the mold, using aluminum tooling for lower volumes, consolidating parts to reduce the number of molds needed, and choosing cost-effective resins that still meet your performance requirements. Working with a shop that does tooling and molding in-house — like Ace's — also eliminates markup from middlemen.
